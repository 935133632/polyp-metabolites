# Plasma NMR metabolomic profiles and incident colon and stomach/duodenal polyps: a prospective analysis of 274,260 UK Biobank participants

## Abstract

**Background.** Polyps of the colon and of the stomach and duodenum differ in aetiology, and the circulating metabolic features that precede them are largely unknown.

**Methods.** We analysed 251 plasma nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) metabolites measured at baseline in 274,260 UK Biobank participants. Incident colon polyp (ICD-10 K63.5; 15,677 cases) and polyp of the stomach and duodenum (K31.7; 7,213 cases) were identified from hospital records over a median follow-up of 13.6 years. Cox models adjusted for age, sex, deprivation, body mass index, blood pressure, smoking and alcohol use, with Bonferroni correction across metabolites. Further analyses covered exclusion of the first two years, additional adjustment for adiposity, HbA1c and C-reactive protein, sex and age subgroups, split-sample and bootstrap validation, comparison with 23 other digestive diseases, a metabolite risk score evaluated in an independent test set, and prediction models.

**Results.** Of 251 metabolites, 157 were associated with colon polyp and 174 with stomach/duodenal polyp; 127 were associated with both, in the same direction. The associated metabolites formed one profile: triglyceride-enriched, cholesterol-depleted lipoproteins, low HDL lipid content (HDL cholesterol HR per SD 0.85 for stomach/duodenal polyp), raised glycoprotein acetyls (HR 1.08 and 1.18) and a lower proportion of linoleic acid (HR 0.89 and 0.90 per SD higher proportion). The associations persisted after excluding early events, and 61% (colon) and 81% (stomach/duodenal) of the main associations remained significant after adjustment for adiposity, HbA1c and C-reactive protein. The profile correlated with that of other digestive diseases (r up to 0.97). In the test set, the highest versus lowest quartile of the metabolite score had a hazard ratio of 1.52 (95% CI 1.39–1.66) for colon polyp and 2.02 (1.76–2.31) for stomach/duodenal polyp. Adding metabolites to clinical covariates raised the C-index from 0.630 to 0.636 and from 0.634 to 0.651.

**Conclusions.** A triglyceride-enriched, HDL-depleted, pro-inflammatory metabolic profile with an unfavourable fatty-acid composition is associated with a higher risk of colon and stomach/duodenal polyps. The profile is shared with other digestive diseases and marks a general metabolic–inflammatory susceptibility, with a clear group-level risk gradient.

**Keywords:** metabolomics; nuclear magnetic resonance; digestive polyps; lipoproteins; glycoprotein acetyls; UK Biobank; cohort study

## Introduction

Polyps of the digestive tract are among the most common findings at endoscopy. Colorectal adenomas and serrated polyps are the precursors of most colorectal cancers, which is why their detection and removal underpin screening and surveillance programmes. Polyps of the stomach and duodenum form a more heterogeneous group: fundic gland polyps often arise during long-term proton-pump-inhibitor use, hyperplastic polyps accompany *Helicobacter pylori* gastritis, and adenomas carry malignant potential [10, 11]. Identifying the systemic factors that precede polyp formation could clarify how polyps arise and which people are at higher risk.

Metabolic health is a leading candidate. Age, male sex, smoking and obesity are established correlates of colorectal adenoma, and meta-analyses link the metabolic syndrome to colorectal adenoma (pooled risk ratio 1.43 [5]; odds ratio 1.39 [6]) and elevated triglycerides to adenoma risk (pooled risk ratio 1.06 [4]). The evidence for HDL cholesterol is weaker and less consistent [4]. These studies rely on routine lipid measures, which summarise heterogeneous lipoprotein classes in a few totals, and little is known about upper gastrointestinal polyps.

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) metabolomics resolves this heterogeneity. A single assay quantifies 249 circulating measures, including the lipid content and composition of lipoprotein subclasses from very large VLDL to small HDL, fatty acids, amino acids, ketone bodies and glycoprotein acetyls, a marker of chronic inflammation [1, 7]. In the UK Biobank these measures have been mapped to hundreds of diseases [1] and related to colorectal cancer [2, 3]. The metabolic profile that precedes polyps, the earlier and more frequent stage, at both ends of the digestive tract has received less attention, and with it the question of which features are shared between sites, which are site-specific, and whether they are particular to polyps or common to digestive disease.

We therefore analysed 251 NMR metabolites measured at baseline in 274,260 UK Biobank participants in relation to incident hospital-diagnosed colon polyp (ICD-10 K63.5) and polyp of the stomach and duodenum (K31.7). We tested whether lipoprotein, fatty-acid and inflammatory metabolites are associated with polyp risk, whether the associations are independent of adiposity, glycaemia and inflammation, how they compare across the two sites and with other digestive diseases, and whether a metabolite score stratifies risk and improves prediction.

## Methods

### Study population and outcomes
We used UK Biobank participants with baseline NMR metabolomic profiling (n = 274,260; 251 metabolite measures). Incident disease was defined from the first hospital inpatient ICD-10 diagnosis date: colon polyp (K63.5) and polyp of the stomach and duodenum (K31.7). Participants with the outcome on or before the baseline date were excluded from the corresponding incident analysis. Follow-up ended at the first event, death or 31 October 2022. The analytic cohorts comprised 272,310 participants with 15,677 incident colon polyps (median follow-up 13.6 years) and 273,634 participants with 7,213 incident stomach/duodenal polyps (median follow-up 13.7 years). As a secondary definition, the original 3-character codes K63 and K31 were also analysed (Fig. S3, Table S1).

### Metabolites and covariates
Each metabolite was rank-based inverse-normal transformed and then standardised within the complete cases of each model, so hazard ratios (HR) are per 1 SD. Models were adjusted for age, sex, Townsend deprivation index, body mass index, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, smoking status and alcohol drinking status; "prefer not to answer" was set to missing. Metabolite abbreviations and their rule-based classes are listed in Table S2.

### Metabolome-wide association analysis
Cox proportional hazards models were fitted for each metabolite and outcome separately. Significance was assessed with Bonferroni correction across the 251 metabolites (P < 0.05/251 = 1.99 × 10⁻⁴) in all analyses. Because the metabolites are highly correlated (about 19–38 effectively independent tests by principal-component and Li-Ji estimates; Fig. S2), Bonferroni correction over 251 metabolites is conservative.

### Additional analyses
Sensitivity analysis excluded events within the first 2 years. Subgroup analyses of all metabolites were run by sex and by age (<60 vs ≥60 years). Internal validation used a 70/30 split-sample replication and 30 bootstrap resamples, both over all 251 metabolites. A baseline companion analysis used adjusted logistic regression for prevalent disease. For participants eligible for both outcomes, groups were classified as colon only, stomach/duodenal only or both, and all 251 metabolites were compared between dual-site and single-site cases by logistic regression.

### Extended adjustment, specificity, score and dose-response
To examine confounding by adiposity, glycaemia and inflammation, the primary models were refitted with additional adjustment for waist circumference and body fat percentage (M2a), HbA1c and log-transformed C-reactive protein (M2b), or all four (M2c), always in the same complete-case sample. To assess specificity, the Model 1 scan of all 251 metabolites was repeated for 23 other digestive diseases with at least 2,000 incident events and for colorectal cancer (ICD-10 C18–C20), and the 251 log hazard ratios of each outcome were correlated with the colon and stomach/duodenal polyp profiles. A metabolite risk score was derived as the metabolite part of a penalised Cox model fitted in the 70% training set and evaluated in the independent 30% test set by hazard ratio per SD, quartiles, a trend test across quartile medians and cumulative incidence. Dose–response was examined with four-knot restricted cubic splines (knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th and 95th percentiles) for the three strongest positive and negative metabolites and for glycoprotein acetyls, HDL cholesterol and total triglycerides, with a likelihood-ratio test against a linear term.

### Prediction
Models used covariates only, covariates plus metabolites passing a Bonferroni-corrected univariate screen run within the training data, or covariates plus all 251 metabolites. Binary prediction used 5-fold stratified cross-validation (logistic regression, elastic net, LightGBM, XGBoost; AUC). Survival prediction used a 70/30 split with a penalised Cox model (penalty chosen on an inner validation split) and an XGBoost Cox model, evaluated by C-index.

## Results

### Cohort
Participants who developed colon polyps were older (58.6 vs 56.4 years), more often male (55.5% vs 45.4%) and had higher BMI (28.4 vs 27.4 kg/m²) than those who did not (Table 1). Those who developed stomach/duodenal polyps were older (59.5 vs 56.5 years) and less often male (39.3% vs 46.2%).

### Metabolome-wide associations
Of 251 metabolites, 157 were associated with incident colon polyp (63 with higher and 94 with lower risk) and 174 with incident stomach/duodenal polyp (68 higher, 106 lower) after Bonferroni correction (Fig. 1, Table S1). Most were lipoprotein-subclass lipid content or composition measures, with both higher and lower risk directions (Fig. 2). For colon polyp, the strongest associations were with the proportion of linoleic acid in total fatty acids (HR 0.89 per SD, 95% CI 0.88–0.91; lower risk), the cholesteryl-ester share of very small VLDL (HR 0.90, 0.88–0.92; lower risk) and the triglyceride share of very small VLDL (HR 1.11, 1.09–1.13; higher risk). For stomach/duodenal polyp, the strongest were cholesteryl esters in very large and large HDL (HR 0.84, 0.81–0.86 and HR 0.83, 0.81–0.85; lower risk) and glycoprotein acetyls (HR 1.18, 1.15–1.21; higher risk) (Fig. 3 shows all 251 metabolites as a circular bar plot for each outcome; Table 2 lists the 20 leading).

### Consistency across outcomes and analyses
Effect estimates for the two outcomes were correlated (r = 0.81); 127 metabolites were significant for both, all in the same direction (Fig. 4). Excluding events in the first 2 years left 141 of the 157 (colon) and 166 of the 174 (stomach/duodenal) main hits significant, all with unchanged direction (Fig. 5C; all 251 metabolites in Fig. S1, Table S1). Among the main hits, the direction of effect was the same in women, men and participants aged 60 or older in 98–100% of cases for both outcomes; in participants under 60 years it was 98% for colon polyp and 91% for stomach/duodenal polyp, the lowest agreement of any stratum (Fig. 5A–B, Table S1). Fifty-one (colon) and 75 (stomach/duodenal) main hits were also significant in all four sex and age strata. In split-sample validation (Fig. S4, Table S1), of the metabolites significant in the discovery half (134 and 149), all had the same direction in the validation half, 95% were nominally significant (P < 0.05) in it, and 41% and 77% also passed Bonferroni correction there; the validation half contained 30% of the sample. In bootstrap resamples (Fig. S4, Table S1), 156 of 157 and 168 of 174 main hits had P < 0.05 in at least 95% of resamples. These checks assess the stability of the associations within the cohort. In baseline logistic models, 75 (colon) and 46 (stomach/duodenal) metabolites were associated with prevalent disease (Fig. S5, Table S1).

### Dual-site polyps
Among eligible participants, 14,497 developed colon polyps only, 5,975 stomach/duodenal polyps only and 1,110 both (Fig. 6A; order of diagnosis in Fig. S7). Comparing participants with both polyps against those with one, one of 251 metabolites remained significant after Bonferroni correction: the phospholipid share of large HDL (OR 1.14 per SD, 95% CI 1.06–1.22). This comparison rests on 1,110 dual-site cases and is hypothesis-generating (Fig. 6B shows all 251 metabolites; Table S1).

### Extended adjustment
In the complete-case sample (240,720 participants with 13,822 colon polyps; 241,891 with 6,370 stomach/duodenal polyps), 145 and 172 metabolites were significant under Model 1. After adding waist circumference and body fat percentage, 117 (81%) and 155 (90%) remained significant; after adding HbA1c and CRP, 122 (84%) and 148 (86%); and after adding all four, 88 (61%) and 139 (81%). All associations kept their direction, and the median attenuation of the log hazard ratio was 26% (colon) and 22% (stomach/duodenal) in the fully adjusted model (Fig. 8, Table S1).

### Specificity
The metabolite profile of both polyp types resembled that of many other digestive diseases (Fig. 9, Tables S4–S5). The correlation between the 251 log hazard ratios for colon polyp and those for another outcome was 0.88–0.93 for 12 of 24 outcomes, including diverticular disease, anorectal diseases, gastritis, liver disease, haemorrhoids and irritable bowel syndrome; for stomach/duodenal polyp it was 0.93–0.97 for reflux, hiatus hernia, gallstones, peritoneal disorders, diverticular disease and irritable bowel syndrome. Colorectal cancer showed a similar but weaker profile (r = 0.76 and 0.57), with 4 metabolites significant after correction. Inguinal hernia had an inverse profile (r = −0.88 and −0.69) and umbilical hernia a weak one (0.43 and 0.56). Glycoprotein acetyls had a positive association with every outcome.

### Metabolite score and dose-response
In the independent test set, the metabolite score was associated with colon polyp (HR per SD 1.19, 95% CI 1.16–1.23; highest vs lowest quartile 1.52, 1.39–1.66; P-trend 2 × 10⁻²²) and with stomach/duodenal polyp (HR per SD 1.34, 1.27–1.40; highest vs lowest quartile 2.02, 1.76–2.31; P-trend 7 × 10⁻²⁵). Fourteen-year cumulative incidence was about 4.1% in the lowest and 8.8% in the highest score quartile for colon polyp, and 1.9% and 3.9% for stomach/duodenal polyp (Fig. 10; score weights in Table S1). Spline curves were mostly monotonic with some curvature at the extremes (nominal P for non-linearity < 0.05 for 7 of 9 and 5 of 8 metabolites; Fig. S8).

### Prediction
Adding metabolites to the covariates changed discrimination little (Fig. 7, Fig. S6, Table S3). For colon polyp, AUC rose from 0.626–0.627 to 0.630–0.631, the C-index from 0.630 to 0.636, and the 5- and 10-year time-dependent AUC from 0.676 to 0.682 and from 0.648 to 0.656. For stomach/duodenal polyp, AUC rose from 0.627–0.632 to 0.636–0.647, the C-index from 0.634–0.635 to 0.647–0.651, and the 5- and 10-year AUC from 0.656 to 0.681 and from 0.650 to 0.667. Overall discrimination remained low (AUC and C-index up to 0.65 in the main analyses; time-dependent AUC up to 0.68), and the Bonferroni-selected set performed like the full set of 251 metabolites.

## Discussion

In 274,260 UK Biobank participants followed for a median of 13.6 years, plasma NMR metabolites were broadly associated with incident polyps: 157 of 251 metabolites for colon polyp (15,677 cases) and 174 for stomach/duodenal polyp (7,213 cases) after Bonferroni correction. The two sites shared 127 associations, all in the same direction (r = 0.81 between effect estimates). The associations held after excluding events in the first two years, in both sexes and age strata, and in split-sample and bootstrap checks. A score built from all metabolites separated risk in an independent test set: the highest quartile had a hazard ratio of 1.5 for colon polyp and 2.0 for stomach/duodenal polyp relative to the lowest, and the 14-year cumulative incidence was 8.8% versus 4.1% for colon polyp and 3.9% versus 1.9% for stomach/duodenal polyp.

The associated metabolites form one coherent profile: triglyceride-enriched, cholesterol-depleted lipoproteins with low HDL lipid content, raised glycoprotein acetyls and an unfavourable fatty-acid composition. Within every lipoprotein class, a higher triglyceride share was associated with higher risk (mean hazard ratio per SD 1.05–1.10 for triglyceride content and 1.06–1.15 for triglyceride percentage) and a higher cholesterol share with lower risk (0.92–0.95 for colon polyp, 0.88–0.92 for stomach/duodenal polyp). Total triglycerides had hazard ratios of 1.06 and 1.10, and HDL cholesterol 0.98 (not significant after correction) and 0.85, with apolipoprotein A1 at 0.89 for stomach/duodenal polyp. The strong HDL association for stomach/duodenal polyp stands against weak or null results for HDL cholesterol and colorectal adenoma in meta-analyses [4]. Glycoprotein acetyls, a marker of chronic inflammation [7], were associated with higher risk (HR 1.08 and 1.18) and albumin with lower risk (0.97 and 0.94). Fatty-acid composition added a second axis: a higher proportion of linoleic acid (HR 0.89 and 0.90) and of polyunsaturated and omega-6 fatty acids was associated with lower risk, and a higher proportion of monounsaturated fatty acids with higher risk (HR 1.09 and 1.16). This matches the inverse association of circulating omega-6 fatty acids and linoleic acid with colorectal cancer in the UK Biobank [8]; dietary linoleic acid intake is positively associated with colorectal cancer [9], and circulating composition integrates diet, adiposity and lipogenesis. The overall pattern resembles the dyslipidaemia of insulin resistance and the metabolic syndrome, which meta-analyses link to colorectal adenoma [4–6]. Adjustment for waist circumference, body fat percentage, HbA1c and CRP reduced the log hazard ratios by a median of 22–26%, and 61% (colon) and 81% (stomach/duodenal) of the main hits remained significant with unchanged direction, so adiposity, glycaemia and inflammation account for part of the profile. Higher LDL cholesterol (HR 0.96 and 0.95) and, for colon polyp, apolipoprotein B (0.96) were associated with slightly lower risk, a pattern that may reflect lipid-lowering treatment and the covariance of these measures with the triglyceride-rich composition.

Many other digestive diseases share the profile. The effect profiles of diverticular disease, gastritis, reflux, gallstones, liver disease and further outcomes correlated with the polyp profiles at r up to 0.97, and glycoprotein acetyls were positively associated with every one of them. Inguinal hernia showed an inverse profile, which marks the limit of the pattern. The profile therefore describes a general metabolic and inflammatory susceptibility to digestive disease, consistent with shared mechanisms such as adiposity, insulin resistance and systemic inflammation, and possibly with shared detection through health-care contact. Within this structure the two polyp types differ in degree: HDL-related measures and glycoprotein acetyls were strongest for stomach/duodenal polyp, and fatty-acid composition and triglyceride proportions in very small VLDL, IDL and LDL for colon polyp. K31.7 pools fundic gland polyps, hyperplastic polyps and adenomas, whose aetiologies differ (acid suppression, *H. pylori*, metabolic factors) [10, 11]. The 1,110 participants with both polyp types differed from single-site cases in one metabolite after correction, the phospholipid share of large HDL (OR 1.14), a finding that awaits replication.

Effect estimates for prevalent and incident disease were highly correlated (r = 0.88 for colon and 0.96 for stomach/duodenal polyp) and persisted after excluding the first two years, which indicates a metabolic state that precedes diagnosis by years. Most dose–response curves were monotonic with curvature at the extremes, for example higher risk only at low HDL cholesterol. The observational design identifies the profile as a marker of risk; Mendelian randomisation or repeated measurements are needed to test whether it contributes causally.

The metabolites stratify risk in groups and add little to individual prediction. Adding them to demographic and clinical covariates raised the AUC by about 0.005 for colon polyp and 0.01–0.03 for stomach/duodenal polyp, with a C-index of at most 0.65 and a time-dependent AUC of at most 0.68, and a feature set selected by Bonferroni within the training data performed like all 251 metabolites.

Strengths of the study are the cohort size, the median follow-up of 13.6 years, a single standardised NMR platform, analyses of all 251 metabolites without pre-selection and consistent results across sensitivity, subgroup, split-sample and bootstrap analyses. The study has one cohort without external replication. Outcomes are hospital diagnoses, so polyps detected at outpatient screening are missed and diagnosis depends on symptoms and endoscopy use, for which the models do not adjust. K63.5 and K31.7 pool histological types. Confounding by diet, physical activity, family history, *H. pylori* and use of proton-pump inhibitors, statins and aspirin remains possible. UK Biobank participants are healthier and less deprived than the general population. Metabolites were measured once, and fasting time was not modelled. The 251 metabolites are collinear (about 19–38 independent tests), so the number of significant metabolites exceeds the number of independent signals. Differences between the two sites were not tested formally.

In conclusion, a triglyceride-enriched, HDL-depleted, pro-inflammatory metabolic profile with an unfavourable fatty-acid composition is associated with a higher risk of colon and stomach/duodenal polyps in the UK Biobank. Adiposity, glycaemia and inflammation explain part of the profile, and the remainder is shared with many other digestive diseases, which makes it a general metabolic–inflammatory susceptibility profile. It gives a clear group-level risk gradient and little individual-level discrimination, and genetic or longitudinal designs are the next step to establish its causal role.

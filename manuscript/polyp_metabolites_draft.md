# Plasma NMR metabolomics and incident colon and stomach/duodenal polyps in the UK Biobank (draft)

## Introduction

Polyps of the digestive tract are common and clinically heterogeneous. Colorectal adenomas and serrated polyps are precursors of colorectal cancer [ref needed: adenoma–carcinoma sequence and screening guidance]. Polyps of the stomach and duodenum comprise several entities with different causes: fundic gland polyps, often arising during long-term proton-pump-inhibitor use, hyperplastic polyps linked to *Helicobacter pylori* gastritis, and adenomas with malignant potential [10, 11]. Established correlates of colorectal adenoma include age, male sex, smoking and obesity, and meta-analyses link the metabolic syndrome to colorectal adenoma (pooled risk ratio 1.43 [5]; odds ratio 1.39 [6]) and elevated triglycerides to adenoma risk (pooled risk ratio 1.06 [4]). Evidence for HDL cholesterol is weaker and less consistent [4], and little is known about the lipoprotein subclass level or about upper gastrointestinal polyps.

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) metabolomics quantifies 249 circulating measures, including lipoprotein subclass lipids, fatty acids, amino acids, ketone bodies and the inflammatory marker glycoprotein acetyls, in a single assay [1]. In the UK Biobank, these measures have been mapped to hundreds of diseases [1] and have been related to colorectal cancer risk [2, 3]. Polyps are an earlier and far more frequent stage than cancer, and a metabolome-wide prospective analysis of polyp incidence at both ends of the digestive tract would show which metabolic features are shared and which are site-specific [ref needed: confirm by a systematic literature search that no such analysis exists].

We therefore examined 251 NMR metabolites measured at baseline in about 274,000 UK Biobank participants in relation to incident hospital-diagnosed colon polyp (ICD-10 K63.5) and polyp of the stomach and duodenum (K31.7), with sensitivity, subgroup, validation, extended-adjustment, specificity, risk-score and prediction analyses.

## Methods

### Study population and outcomes
We used UK Biobank participants with baseline NMR metabolomic profiling (n = 274,260; 251 metabolite measures). Incident disease was defined from the first hospital inpatient ICD-10 diagnosis date: colon polyp (K63.5) and polyp of the stomach and duodenum (K31.7). Participants with the outcome on or before the baseline date were excluded from the corresponding incident analysis. Follow-up ended at the first event, death or 31 October 2022. The analytic cohorts comprised 272,310 participants with 15,677 incident colon polyps (median follow-up 13.6 years) and 273,634 participants with 7,213 incident stomach/duodenal polyps (median follow-up 13.7 years). As a secondary definition, the original 3-character codes K63 and K31 were also analysed (Fig. S3, Table S9).

### Metabolites and covariates
Each metabolite was rank-based inverse-normal transformed and then standardised within the complete cases of each model, so hazard ratios (HR) are per 1 SD. Models were adjusted for age, sex, Townsend deprivation index, body mass index, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, smoking status and alcohol drinking status; "prefer not to answer" was set to missing. Metabolite abbreviations and their rule-based classes are listed in Table S12.

### Metabolome-wide association analysis
Cox proportional hazards models were fitted for each metabolite and outcome separately. Significance was assessed with Bonferroni correction across the 251 metabolites (P < 0.05/251 = 1.99 × 10⁻⁴) in all analyses. Because the metabolites are highly correlated (about 19–38 effectively independent tests by principal-component and Li-Ji estimates; Fig. S2), this correction is conservative, and the number of significant metabolites does not equal the number of independent signals.

### Additional analyses
Sensitivity analysis excluded events within the first 2 years. Subgroup analyses of all metabolites were run by sex and by age (<60 vs ≥60 years; <65 vs ≥65 as a supplementary cut-off). Internal validation used a 70/30 split-sample replication and 30 bootstrap resamples, both over all 251 metabolites. A baseline companion analysis used adjusted logistic regression for prevalent disease. For participants eligible for both outcomes, groups were classified as colon only, stomach/duodenal only or both, and all 251 metabolites were compared between dual-site and single-site cases by logistic regression.

### Extended adjustment, specificity, score and dose-response
To examine confounding by adiposity, glycaemia and inflammation, the primary models were refitted with additional adjustment for waist circumference and body fat percentage (M2a), HbA1c and log-transformed C-reactive protein (M2b), or all four (M2c), always in the same complete-case sample. To assess specificity, the Model 1 scan of all 251 metabolites was repeated for 23 other digestive diseases with at least 2,000 incident events and for colorectal cancer (ICD-10 C18–C20), and the 251 log hazard ratios of each outcome were correlated with the colon and stomach/duodenal polyp profiles. A metabolite risk score was derived as the metabolite part of a penalised Cox model fitted in the 70% training set and evaluated in the independent 30% test set by hazard ratio per SD, quartiles, a trend test across quartile medians and cumulative incidence. Dose–response was examined with four-knot restricted cubic splines (knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th and 95th percentiles) for the three strongest positive and negative metabolites and for glycoprotein acetyls, HDL cholesterol and total triglycerides, with a likelihood-ratio test against a linear term.

### Prediction
Models used covariates only, covariates plus metabolites passing a Bonferroni-corrected univariate screen run within the training data, or covariates plus all 251 metabolites. Binary prediction used 5-fold stratified cross-validation (logistic regression, elastic net, LightGBM, XGBoost; AUC). Survival prediction used a 70/30 split with a penalised Cox model (penalty chosen on an inner validation split) and an XGBoost Cox model, evaluated by C-index.

## Results

### Cohort
Participants who developed colon polyps were older (58.6 vs 56.4 years), more often male (55.5% vs 45.4%) and had higher BMI (28.4 vs 27.4 kg/m²) than those who did not (Table 1; Table S13). Those who developed stomach/duodenal polyps were older (59.5 vs 56.5 years) and less often male (39.3% vs 46.2%).

### Metabolome-wide associations
Of 251 metabolites, 157 were associated with incident colon polyp (63 with higher and 94 with lower risk) and 174 with incident stomach/duodenal polyp (68 higher, 106 lower) after Bonferroni correction (Fig. 1, Table S1). Most were lipoprotein-subclass lipid content or composition measures, with both higher and lower risk directions (Fig. 2). For colon polyp, the strongest associations were with the proportion of linoleic acid in total fatty acids (HR 0.89 per SD, 95% CI 0.88–0.91; lower risk), the cholesteryl-ester share of very small VLDL (HR 0.90, 0.88–0.92; lower risk) and the triglyceride share of very small VLDL (HR 1.11, 1.09–1.13; higher risk). For stomach/duodenal polyp, the strongest were cholesteryl esters in very large and large HDL (HR 0.84, 0.81–0.86 and HR 0.83, 0.81–0.85; lower risk) and glycoprotein acetyls (HR 1.18, 1.15–1.21; higher risk) (Fig. 3 shows all 251 metabolites as a circular bar plot for each outcome; Table 2 lists the 20 leading).

### Consistency across outcomes and analyses
Effect estimates for the two outcomes were correlated (r = 0.81); 127 metabolites were significant for both, all in the same direction (Fig. 4). Excluding events in the first 2 years left 141 of the 157 (colon) and 166 of the 174 (stomach/duodenal) main hits significant, all with unchanged direction (Fig. 5C; all 251 metabolites in Fig. S1, Table S2). Among the main hits, the direction of effect was the same in women, men and participants aged 60 or older in 98–100% of cases for both outcomes; in participants under 60 years it was 98% for colon polyp and 91% for stomach/duodenal polyp, the lowest agreement of any stratum (65-year cut-off: 99–100%; Fig. 5A–B, Fig. S5, Tables S3–S4). Fifty-one (colon) and 75 (stomach/duodenal) main hits were also significant in all four sex and age strata. In split-sample validation (Fig. S4, Table S6), of the metabolites significant in the discovery half (134 and 149), all had the same direction in the validation half, 95% were nominally significant (P < 0.05) in it, and 41% and 77% also passed Bonferroni correction there; the validation half contained 30% of the sample. In bootstrap resamples (Fig. S4, Table S7), 156 of 157 and 168 of 174 main hits had P < 0.05 in at least 95% of resamples. These checks show stability within one cohort, not independent replication. In baseline logistic models, 75 (colon) and 46 (stomach/duodenal) metabolites were associated with prevalent disease (Fig. S6, Table S5).

### Dual-site polyps
Among eligible participants, 14,497 developed colon polyps only, 5,975 stomach/duodenal polyps only and 1,110 both (Fig. 6A; order of diagnosis in Fig. S8). Comparing participants with both polyps against those with one, one of 251 metabolites remained significant after Bonferroni correction: the phospholipid share of large HDL (OR 1.14 per SD, 95% CI 1.06–1.22). This comparison rests on 1,110 dual-site cases and is hypothesis-generating (Fig. 6B shows all 251 metabolites; Table S8).

### Extended adjustment
In the complete-case sample (240,720 participants with 13,822 colon polyps; 241,891 with 6,370 stomach/duodenal polyps), 145 and 172 metabolites were significant under Model 1. After adding waist circumference and body fat percentage, 117 (81%) and 155 (90%) remained significant; after adding HbA1c and CRP, 122 (84%) and 148 (86%); and after adding all four, 88 (61%) and 139 (81%). All associations kept their direction, and the median attenuation of the log hazard ratio was 26% (colon) and 22% (stomach/duodenal) in the fully adjusted model (Fig. 8, Tables S14–S15).

### Specificity
The metabolite profile of both polyp types resembled that of many other digestive diseases (Fig. 9, Tables S16–S17). The correlation between the 251 log hazard ratios for colon polyp and those for another outcome was 0.88–0.93 for 12 of 24 outcomes, including diverticular disease, anorectal diseases, gastritis, liver disease, haemorrhoids and irritable bowel syndrome; for stomach/duodenal polyp it was 0.93–0.97 for reflux, hiatus hernia, gallstones, peritoneal disorders, diverticular disease and irritable bowel syndrome. Colorectal cancer showed a similar but weaker profile (r = 0.76 and 0.57), with 4 metabolites significant after correction. Inguinal hernia had an inverse profile (r = −0.88 and −0.69) and umbilical hernia a weak one (0.43 and 0.56). Glycoprotein acetyls had a positive association with every outcome.

### Metabolite score and dose-response
In the independent test set, the metabolite score was associated with colon polyp (HR per SD 1.19, 95% CI 1.16–1.23; highest vs lowest quartile 1.52, 1.39–1.66; P-trend 2 × 10⁻²²) and with stomach/duodenal polyp (HR per SD 1.34, 1.27–1.40; highest vs lowest quartile 2.02, 1.76–2.31; P-trend 7 × 10⁻²⁵). Fourteen-year cumulative incidence was about 4.1% in the lowest and 8.8% in the highest score quartile for colon polyp, and 1.9% and 3.9% for stomach/duodenal polyp (Fig. 10, Tables S18–S19). Spline curves were mostly monotonic with some curvature at the extremes (nominal P for non-linearity < 0.05 for 7 of 9 and 5 of 8 metabolites; Fig. S9, Tables S20–S21).

### Prediction
Adding metabolites to the covariates changed discrimination little (Fig. 7, Fig. S7, Tables S10–S11). For colon polyp, AUC rose from 0.626–0.627 to 0.630–0.631, the C-index from 0.630 to 0.636, and the 5- and 10-year time-dependent AUC from 0.676 to 0.682 and from 0.648 to 0.656. For stomach/duodenal polyp, AUC rose from 0.627–0.632 to 0.636–0.647, the C-index from 0.634–0.635 to 0.647–0.651, and the 5- and 10-year AUC from 0.656 to 0.681 and from 0.650 to 0.667. Overall discrimination remained low (AUC and C-index up to 0.65 in the main analyses; time-dependent AUC up to 0.68), and the Bonferroni-selected set performed like the full set of 251 metabolites.

## Discussion

In this prospective analysis of 274,260 UK Biobank participants, 157 of 251 NMR metabolites were associated with incident colon polyp and 174 with incident stomach/duodenal polyp after Bonferroni correction. The associations were consistent across the two sites (127 shared metabolites, all with the same direction; r = 0.81 between effect estimates), were not explained by events in the first two years, and were present in both sexes and across age strata. They were dominated by lipoprotein-subclass lipid measures and had a characteristic shape: lower risk with higher HDL lipid content and higher proportions of cholesterol within lipoprotein subclasses, and higher risk with higher triglyceride content and triglyceride proportions, higher glycoprotein acetyls, and a less favourable fatty-acid profile. The profile was only partly explained by adiposity, glycaemia and inflammation, and it was shared with many other digestive diseases.

**Lipoproteins and triglycerides.** Within each lipoprotein class, a higher triglyceride share was associated with higher risk (mean hazard ratio per SD 1.05–1.10 for the triglyceride content of VLDL, IDL, LDL and HDL, and 1.06–1.15 for triglyceride percentage) and a higher cholesterol share with lower risk (0.92–0.95 for colon polyp, 0.88–0.92 for stomach/duodenal polyp). Total triglycerides had hazard ratios of 1.06 (colon) and 1.10 (stomach/duodenal). This pattern of triglyceride-enriched, cholesterol-depleted lipoproteins resembles the dyslipidaemia of insulin resistance and the metabolic syndrome, in agreement with meta-analytic evidence linking the metabolic syndrome and triglycerides to colorectal adenoma [4–6]. HDL lipid content was strongly inverse for stomach/duodenal polyp (HDL cholesterol HR 0.85; apolipoprotein A1 HR 0.89) but weaker for colon polyp (HDL cholesterol HR 0.98, not significant after correction). Meta-analyses of HDL cholesterol and colorectal adenoma were also weak or null [4], so the site difference is of interest, but we did not test it formally. Higher LDL cholesterol (HR 0.96 and 0.95) and, for colon polyp, apolipoprotein B (HR 0.96) were associated with slightly lower risk, which is counter-intuitive; use of lipid-lowering drugs, which we could not adjust for, or the correlation of these measures with triglyceride-rich particle composition may contribute. A UK Biobank analysis of about 380,000 adults reported no association of blood lipid biomarkers with colorectal cancer after adjustment for adiposity [ref needed: identify study]. Our models adjust for BMI, yet most lipoprotein associations remained, which could reflect the earlier disease stage studied here or adiposity not captured by BMI.

**Fatty acids.** A higher proportion of linoleic acid (HR 0.89 and 0.90) and of polyunsaturated and omega-6 fatty acids was associated with lower risk, whereas higher proportions of monounsaturated fatty acids (HR 1.09 and 1.16) and, for colon polyp, saturated fatty acids were associated with higher risk. Higher circulating omega-6 fatty acids and linoleic acid were associated with lower colorectal cancer risk in the UK Biobank [8], in line with our findings, whereas higher dietary linoleic acid intake was associated with higher colorectal cancer risk in a meta-analysis of cohorts [9]. Circulating fatty-acid composition reflects diet, adiposity, lipogenesis and lipid metabolism, so it should not be read as an effect of dietary intake.

**Inflammation.** Glycoprotein acetyls, a marker of chronic systemic inflammation [7], were associated with higher risk (HR 1.08 and 1.18), and albumin, a negative acute-phase protein, with lower risk (HR 0.97 and 0.94). This is compatible with a role for low-grade inflammation, although the metabolic and inflammatory signals are intertwined with adiposity and cannot be separated here; glycoprotein acetyls were also associated with every other digestive outcome we examined (see Specificity).

**Differences between the two sites.** The two profiles were similar overall (r = 0.81), but the strongest signals differed: HDL-related measures and glycoprotein acetyls for stomach/duodenal polyp, and fatty-acid composition and triglyceride proportions in very small VLDL, IDL and LDL for colon polyp. K31.7 includes fundic gland polyps, hyperplastic polyps and adenomas, whose aetiologies differ (acid suppression, *H. pylori*, metabolic factors) [10, 11], and the hospital code does not distinguish them. Participants with both polyp types (n = 1,110) were few, and only one metabolite (the phospholipid share of large HDL) differed from single-site cases after Bonferroni correction, so this comparison is hypothesis-generating.

**Timing and interpretation.** Effect estimates for prevalent and incident disease were highly correlated (r = 0.88 for colon and 0.96 for stomach/duodenal polyp), and the associations persisted after excluding events in the first two years. A metabolic profile present long before diagnosis is compatible with a persistent susceptibility state, but this analysis cannot separate a cause of polyp formation from a shared consequence of obesity, insulin resistance or diet. Mendelian randomisation or repeated metabolite measures would be needed to address causality; we did neither.

**Specificity.** The metabolic profile was not specific to polyps. Effect profiles for diverticular disease, gastritis, reflux, gallstones, liver disease and several other digestive outcomes were closely correlated with the polyp profiles (r up to 0.97), and glycoprotein acetyls were positively associated with all of them. Inguinal hernia, in contrast, showed an inverse profile, so the pattern is not shared by every outcome. These findings suggest that the triglyceride-enriched, HDL-depleted, pro-inflammatory profile marks a general metabolic and inflammatory susceptibility to digestive disease, rather than a feature of polyp biology; shared mechanisms such as adiposity, insulin resistance and systemic inflammation, or shared detection through health-care contact, are plausible. The two polyp profiles correlate with each other (r = 0.81) less closely than each correlates with some other outcomes (for example r = 0.97 between the stomach/duodenal polyp profile and reflux), so the site-specific features described above should be read as differences in degree.

**Confounding by adiposity, glycaemia and inflammation.** Adjustment for waist circumference, body fat percentage, HbA1c and CRP reduced the log hazard ratios by a median of 22–26%, and 61% (colon) and 81% (stomach/duodenal) of the main hits stayed significant with unchanged direction. The associations are therefore not explained by these measures alone, but a substantial share is related to them, as expected for a metabolic profile of this kind. Use of lipid-lowering drugs, aspirin and proton-pump inhibitors could not be adjusted.

**Score and dose-response.** A metabolite score derived in a training set separated risk groups in an independent test set (highest vs lowest quartile HR 1.5 for colon polyp and 2.0 for stomach/duodenal polyp), while its contribution to prediction beyond clinical covariates remained small (see Prediction); a clear group-level gradient is compatible with weak individual-level discrimination. Most dose–response curves were monotonic, with curvature at the extremes (for example higher risk at low HDL cholesterol only).

**Prediction.** Adding metabolites to demographic and clinical covariates improved discrimination by about 0.005 (colon) and 0.01–0.03 (stomach/duodenal) in AUC (C-index at most 0.65; time-dependent AUC at most 0.68), and a feature set selected by Bonferroni within the training data performed like all 251 metabolites. The metabolites are informative about risk in groups but not useful for predicting polyps in individuals.

**Strengths and limitations.** Strengths are the large cohort, long follow-up (median 13.6 years), a single standardised NMR platform, analyses of all 251 metabolites without pre-selection, and consistent results across sensitivity, subgroup, split-sample and bootstrap analyses. Limitations: (1) one cohort without external validation, and the split-sample and bootstrap checks show stability, not replication; (2) outcomes are hospital diagnoses, so polyps found at outpatient screening are missed and diagnosis depends on symptoms and endoscopy use, which we could not adjust for; (3) K63.5 and K31.7 pool histological types; (4) residual confounding by diet, physical activity, family history, *H. pylori*, and proton-pump-inhibitor, statin and aspirin use (adiposity, HbA1c and CRP were adjusted in a sensitivity analysis); (5) UK Biobank participants are healthier and less deprived than the general population; (6) metabolites were measured once and fasting time was not modelled; (7) the 251 metabolites are highly collinear (about 19–38 independent tests), so the number of significant metabolites overstates the number of independent signals; (8) the grouping of a few metabolite abbreviations in the circular figures is rule-based and should be checked against the data dictionary.

**Conclusion.** A triglyceride-enriched, HDL-depleted, pro-inflammatory metabolic profile with an unfavourable fatty-acid composition was associated with a higher risk of both colon and stomach/duodenal polyps in the UK Biobank. The profile was only partly explained by adiposity, glycaemia and inflammation, but it was shared with many other digestive diseases, so it is best interpreted as a general metabolic-inflammatory risk profile rather than a polyp-specific signature. The associations are robust within this cohort and give a clear group-level risk gradient, but they add little to individual risk prediction, and their causal role remains to be tested.
